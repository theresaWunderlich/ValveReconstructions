Quantifying cellular organization in a congenital heart disease model

In Tetralogy of Fallot, blood is forced through the heart along altered paths. In a chick model of the disease, the pulmonary valve lost cell material and gained extracellular matrix, while the aortic valve stayed essentially unchanged: a valve-specific pattern consistent with blood flow shaping how valves remodel. The 3D reconstructions below are the volumes behind that result.

The pulmonary valve changed; the aortic valve didn't. The ToF pulmonary leaflet had fewer cells and more of the material between them. That flow-specific pattern is what the hypothesis predicted.

Nuclei: a hint, not yet a result. The share of the volume taken up by nuclei was similar (0.080 vs. 0.089), and with one sample per condition that difference doesn't mean much on its own. In the renders, though, control nuclei look fewer but larger, while ToF nuclei look more numerous and smaller, which could mean the ToF valve is built from smaller, less mature cells. Counting individual nuclei is how I'm testing that.

Background

Congenital heart disease (CHD) means a baby is born with a defect in the heart. It affects about 1% of newborns worldwide, and in the United States it is the leading non-infectious cause of death in infants and children.

Tetralogy of Fallot (ToF) is one of the most common forms. Heart defects can form when blood flow is altered during early development, and in ToF the blood moves through the aorta at higher speed than normal. ToF is defined by four features:

01

Ventricular septal defect

A hole in the wall between the two lower chambers.

02

Pulmonary stenosis

The pulmonary valve is narrowed.

03

Overriding aorta

The aorta sits over both ventricles instead of over the left ventricle only.

04

Right ventricular hypertrophy

The muscle of the right ventricle thickens.

Two of these reshape the flow. Think of a garden hose: squeeze the opening and the water comes out faster. Pulmonary stenosis does that to blood. Add the hole between the ventricles, and blood can take the easier path, rerouting into the aorta.

Hypothesis: the altered blood flow in a ToF heart changes the cellular structure inside the valves.

Aim: to better understand how blood flow affects the way heart valve leaflets develop and mature. Chick embryos treated with cyclopamine develop ToF hearts, with increased flow through the aortic valve and abnormal remodeling. To see and measure the difference between ToF and normal valves at the cellular level, I trained deep-learning models to segment cell structures in volume electron microscopy.

04

Methods

Sample collection

Chick embryos are a well-established model for human heart development. They are easy to reach and manipulate inside the shell, so the developing heart can be studied with minimal interference.

Intervene

Add cyclopamine to chick embryos to induce the ToF phenotype.

Confirm phenotype

In vivo ultrasound imaging of blood flow verifies the ToF heart.

Collect

Collect the embryos and hearts.

Prepare

Prepare samples for electron microscopy.

Electron microscopy

Electron microscopy uses a beam of electrons to make extremely detailed, high-magnification images. Image stacks were collected from cropped regions of interest (ROIs) in each heart.

Deep-learning segmentation

All segmentation ran in Dragonfly ORS. The workflow has four steps: label ground truth, train the model, apply it to the whole stack, and measure by counting voxels in each class.

To teach the model, I first gave it an answer key: structures traced by hand on selected slices (ground-truth slices). Early on these were close together, about every 20 slices, so the model always had a nearby example. As it improved I spread them to 40–60 slices apart, because it had learned to fill the bigger gaps on its own. Each training session ran 100 epochs.

early: ~20 slices apartlater: 40–60 slices apart

Once trained, the model ran across the whole image stack, the result was rebuilt in 3D, and the amount of each class was counted.

Both models are U-Nets. A U-Net labels every point in an image by what it is (cell, nucleus, or the material between cells). It works in two passes: first it zooms out to work out what is in the image, then it zooms back in to mark where each piece is. Drawn out, that path looks like the letter U.

U-Net

Cell material vs. extracellular matrix.

U-Net++

Nuclei. A more detailed variant, because nuclei are smaller and harder to catch. Nuclei were carved out of the cell class so the cell/matrix split stays fixed.

All reported results come from a uniform 40 × 40 µm square taken from the exact middle of each ROI.

05

Conclusions

  • Blood flow appears to shape valve remodeling.The change is specific to one valve, not spread across the heart.
  • The obstructed pulmonary valve changed most.Fewer cells and more extracellular matrix in ToF; the aortic valve stayed essentially the same.
  • Nuclei may differ.Control nuclei look fewer and larger, ToF nuclei more numerous and smaller. Nuclei volume fraction alone doesn't settle it, so counting is next.
06

What's next

  • Preprocessing in progressEverything here ran on raw EM with no cleanup. A step that evens out brightness and noise, similar to the preprocessing used for watershed segmentation, should make segmentation more accurate and test whether these findings hold.
  • Cell counting in progressMoving from how much volume nuclei fill to how many individual nuclei appear in each valve. A model derived from the nuclei segmentation now colors and labels each nucleus separately (see the single-nucleus reconstructions above).
  • Nucleoplasmic reticulumFolds of the nuclear envelope that reach into the nucleus. How much folding there is tracks with cell state: extensive networks appear in aging cells, stressed cells and some cancers. In heart cells these folds sit near the calcium-signaling machinery that helps tell cells how to grow and function. If the amount differs between valves and hearts, that is a measurable fingerprint of altered cell organization in disease.
  • Whole image slicesOnly the interior of each ROI has been analyzed so far. Extending to whole slices would characterize the full valve, not one central crop.
  • More samplesEach comparison here is one ROI per condition. More hearts are needed before any difference can be tested statistically.
